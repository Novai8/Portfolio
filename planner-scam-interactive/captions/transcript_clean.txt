I need you to imagine this: it's 2:07 a.m., my laptop is balanced on a laundry basket, and I'm eating cereal out of a coffee mug… because I'm an entrepreneur now. I had just launched my first "real" business: a tiny online shop selling custom productivity planners for freelancers. Nothing fancy—just clean designs, funny prompts, and the occasional passive-aggressive sticker like "Did you actually send that invoice?" I refresh my store dashboard one more time, expecting the usual: one order, maybe two if my mom gets excited. Then my screen updates. And I see it. A single order. For 500 planners. Total: $12,480. I just stare at it like it's going to apologize and turn into a typo. "Five… hundred?" My heart starts doing parkour in my chest. I'm not a business owner. I'm a person with Wi‑Fi and a dream. I click into the details, already mentally spending the profit on adult things like… a chair with lumbar support. The customer name is "Elliot Graye." The email looks legit. The payment cleared. And then I see the shipping address. It's an industrial street on the edge of town. No suite number. No business name. Just a street address. I zoom in on the map. It's… an abandoned warehouse. Not "cool startup loft" warehouse. Like, "a raccoon probably pays rent here" warehouse. I feel the excitement drain out of me like someone pulled the plug. Because there are only two possibilities: One: a mysterious billionaire loves planners. Two: I am about to get spectacularly scammed. Okay. Three possibilities: maybe the raccoon is the billionaire. I email the customer. "Hey Elliot! Huge thanks for your order—just confirming delivery details for such a large shipment." Five minutes later, a reply pops in. "Ship ASAP. Need by Friday. Don't call. Busy." And then, like a cherry on top of the stress sundae: "Also send tracking immediately." My brain starts listing every horror story I've ever read: chargebacks, fake cards, "delivered" disputes, the classic "I never received it" performance. I walk to my living room, which is also my office, which is also my dining room. And I do the thing every entrepreneur does when faced with danger: I call my friend Maya. Maya picks up on the second ring. "If this is about your plants again, I told you: talking to them doesn't count as marketing." "Maya. I got a twelve-thousand-dollar order. And it's going to an abandoned warehouse." There's a beat. "Okay. Do not ship that." "But what if it's real?" "Then they can confirm the address like a normal human and not like a movie villain." I hang up and sit back down, trying to breathe like a calm person who definitely doesn't own three "How to Start a Business" books and zero common sense. I decide to do a little digging. First: I Google the address. Nothing. Second: I search the name "Elliot Graye." I get an actor. A dentist. A guy selling used jet skis. Third: I search the email. It's linked to… nothing. My stomach drops. This is the part where horror movies say, "Don't open the door." Then I get another email. "Why no tracking yet?" It's been fourteen minutes since the order. I reply, keeping it polite. "Hi! For high-volume orders, we confirm shipping details for security. Could you tell me the business name at the delivery location and a contact number?" The response arrives instantly. "No time. Ship now. I paid." Now my hands are cold. That's not a customer. That's a countdown. I stand up, pace in a tiny circle, and then I do something I've never done in my life. I call the number on the payment processor. After being serenaded by hold music that sounds like a depressed elevator, a rep answers. "Hi—sorry—this might sound weird, but can you tell me if this payment looks risky? Big order, sketchy address." The rep goes quiet. "I can't confirm details… but I can tell you this: the transaction is marked 'high risk.'" My face goes hot. "So… scam." "It could be fraud, yes." I thank them, hang up, and stare at my screen like it betrayed me personally. Then another email hits. "If you don't ship in 1 hour I will report you. Refund me now." I blink. Wait. They want a refund now? That's when it clicks—like a puzzle piece snapping into place. This isn't about planners. It's about pressure. They want me to panic and hit "refund," and maybe they've already set up some trick where the money goes somewhere else. Or they want proof of shipment so they can win a dispute. Either way, I'm not becoming a cautionary tale on the internet. I type back. "Thanks for your message. Due to security policy, we require address verification for high-value orders. If you prefer, I can cancel and the payment will be voided." Not refunded. Voided. Two minutes pass. Then I get a reply that is… unhinged. "YOU WILL SHIP. I KNOW WHERE YOU LIVE." I laugh, but it comes out like fear wearing a comedy mask. Because—no, you don't. But also—WHY ARE YOU YELLING? I take a screenshot. Then another. Then I cancel the order through the processor as a suspected fraud attempt. The dashboard updates: cancelled. Funds reversed. No shipment. I should feel relieved. But instead, I feel… shaky. Like I just stepped off a moving train. And that's when I notice something else. My site traffic spikes. Like, a lot. I open analytics. Hundreds of visitors… all hitting my checkout page. Over and over. My throat tightens. They're testing my store. Trying different cards. Different names. Seeing what sticks. I whisper to myself: "Oh my god, I'm a vending machine to them." I do the fastest "entrepreneur bootcamp" of my life in the next twenty minutes: I turn on stricter fraud filters. I block certain regions. I add address verification. I disable bulk quantities temporarily. I add a captcha. I breathe. Then, finally, the traffic drops. Silence returns. I sit there, hands on the keyboard, and I start laughing—real laughing. Not because it's funny… but because I genuinely thought entrepreneurship was going to be, like, choosing brand colors and posting motivational quotes. Turns out it's also occasionally wrestling a cyber-goblin at 2 a.m. in pajama pants. The next morning, I wake up to one new email. Subject line: "Order Inquiry (Legit)" I open it cautiously. "Hi! I run a coworking space. We want 200 planners with our logo. Can we talk?" I stare at it. My eye twitches. "Sure. Totally. Let me just recover from last night's villain arc." We hop on a call. They're real. They're friendly. They laugh at my jokes. They have a website and a phone number and—get this—a normal address with a front door. I fulfill the order. It goes smoothly. They reorder the next month. And here's the twist: That scam attempt? It didn't just scare me. It upgraded me. Because I realized something important: if my tiny little planner shop could attract fraudsters, it meant I'd built something real enough to be targeted. So I changed how I ran my business. I built simple security rules. I wrote policies in plain English. I stopped worshipping "big numbers" and started verifying reality. And the funniest part? A few months later, a friend asked, "How did you learn fraud prevention so fast?" I said, "By almost donating twelve thousand dollars worth of planners to a raccoon warehouse." Look—if you're building something, the wins feel amazing. But the near-misses? Those are the moments you actually become the person who can handle the wins. And if you ever get a massive order at 2:07 a.m. to an abandoned warehouse… Don't ship it. Ship your spine first.
